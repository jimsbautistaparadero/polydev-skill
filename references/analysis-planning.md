# Data Management and Analysis Planning

## 1. Plan analysis before collecting data

For each research question, define the evidence required, source, variable/construct, instrument field, analysis procedure, output, and interpretation boundary. If analysis cannot be planned, the question or instrument is not yet operational.

## 2. Quantitative analysis map

| Purpose | Common analysis | Checks and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Describe categories | Counts, proportions | Denominator, missingness, small cells |
| Describe numeric values | Mean/SD or median/IQR, range | Distribution, outliers, scale and skew |
| Compare two groups | Appropriate t-test or non-parametric alternative; paired methods for paired data | Independence, distribution, variance, pairing, design |
| Compare multiple groups | ANOVA or suitable alternatives | Assumptions, post-hoc comparisons, multiplicity |
| Association between categorical variables | Chi-square or exact methods | Expected counts, independence |
| Association between numeric/ordinal variables | Correlation or regression suited to scale | Linearity, outliers, confounding, measurement |
| Prediction/adjustment | Regression model appropriate to outcome | Model specification, sample adequacy, diagnostics, calibration |
| Reliability | Appropriate internal consistency or agreement statistic | Dimensionality and assumptions; do not report alpha mechanically |

This is a decision aid, not an automatic method picker. Select methods based on the estimand, design, measurement, sampling, and assumptions. Report uncertainty and effect magnitude where appropriate.

## 3. Qualitative analysis

State the analytic approach before data collection. For thematic analysis, for example, describe familiarization, initial coding, theme construction/review, definition, and reporting. For framework or content analysis, describe the coding framework, unit of analysis, codebook, coder training, disagreement resolution, and treatment of emergent codes.

Keep an audit trail linking excerpts or observations to codes, categories, themes, and interpretations. Use quotations responsibly and remove identifying details. Do not invent quotations or imply a theme was participant-endorsed if the evidence does not show that.

## 4. Mixed-methods integration

Specify integration through connecting, building, merging, or embedding. Useful displays include joint tables that place quantitative findings beside qualitative explanations. Explain convergence, complementarity, expansion, and disagreement. Do not simply report two independent sets of results and call that integration.

## 5. Data lifecycle

1. Define data inventory and owners.
2. Obtain approvals and informed consent as required.
3. Assign study IDs; keep any re-identification key separate and access-restricted.
4. Preserve raw data and metadata securely.
5. Create a data dictionary and data-cleaning log.
6. Validate ranges, types, duplicates, missingness, and protocol deviations.
7. Document exclusions and transformations.
8. Analyze using reproducible scripts or written procedures.
9. Preserve code, software/version details, outputs, and analysis decisions.
10. Retain or delete data according to approved policy and consent.

## 6. Missing data, outliers, and deviations

Do not silently delete incomplete records, extreme values, or inconvenient responses. Quantify missingness, inspect patterns, identify likely causes, and choose a justified treatment. Distinguish data errors from genuine unusual observations. Record protocol deviations and assess their effect on interpretation.

## 7. Interpretation discipline

- Statistical significance is not practical importance.
- Correlation does not establish causation.
- A small or convenience sample limits inference.
- A user satisfaction score does not prove efficiency or service impact.
- A functional test does not prove usability.
- A successful demo does not prove production reliability.
- A pre/post difference without a credible counterfactual may not identify the intervention's causal effect.
- Qualitative themes describe patterns in the studied data; they are not automatically population prevalence estimates.

## 8. Reporting outputs

For each question, report the method, sample/data basis, assumptions/checks, result, uncertainty/limitations, and interpretation. Tables and figures should have clear titles, labels, denominators, units, and source notes. Keep analysis outputs reproducible and separate from draft narrative.
