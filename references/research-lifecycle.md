# Research Lifecycle and Study Architecture

This guide expands the lifecycle in `SKILL.md`. Use it to keep the study coherent from inception to handoff.

## 1. The logic chain

A defensible study has a traceable chain:

`Context → Demonstrated problem → Knowledge/practice gap → Purpose → Objectives → Research questions → Framework → Design → Participants/data → Instruments/procedures → Analysis → Findings → Conclusions → Recommendations`

For each link, ask:

- What evidence supports this link?
- What artifact records it?
- What must be true before the next link can be designed?
- What would make the link invalid?

A break in the chain is a design defect, not merely a writing issue.

## 2. Study classification

Classify the study on more than one dimension. A system-development capstone, for example, can be applied research, design science, mixed-methods evaluation, or a descriptive requirements study depending on its questions and evidence.

| Dimension | Common options | Decision test |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Exploratory, descriptive, explanatory, evaluative, predictive, design-oriented | What knowledge or decision will the study produce? |
| Evidence | Qualitative, quantitative, mixed methods, documentary/secondary | What evidence can answer the questions? |
| Time | Cross-sectional, longitudinal, retrospective | When must evidence be observed? |
| Intervention | Observational, quasi-experimental, experimental, design/evaluation | Is the research deliberately changing a condition? |
| Contribution | Theory, empirical, methodological, practical/design, contextual | What will be new or useful beyond the artifact itself? |
| Review | Narrative, scoping, systematic, integrative, rapid | What review question and rigor are required? |

Do not force a single label where the project has multiple phases. Describe the phase-specific design and integration logic.

## 3. Study brief content

The study brief is the source of truth for current scope. It should contain:

1. Working title and version/date.
2. Discipline, academic level, and study type.
3. Context and stakeholders.
4. Problem as currently understood and evidence for it.
5. Proposed contribution and beneficiaries.
6. Aim, draft objectives, and draft questions.
7. Setting, population/unit of analysis, and boundaries.
8. Candidate framework and method, with confidence labels.
9. Required approvals, constraints, resources, and deadline.
10. Existing artifacts and decisions.
11. Open questions and assumptions.

The brief is not a final proposal. It is a controlled snapshot to prevent drift.

## 4. Feasibility analysis

Evaluate each dimension as `adequate`, `constrained`, or `unknown` and explain the evidence:

- Problem access: can the team observe or document the problem?
- Participant/data access: can the target population or dataset be accessed lawfully?
- Permission: are gatekeeper and ethics approvals obtainable in time?
- Method capability: can the team competently execute the method?
- Measurement: can the proposed outcomes be measured credibly?
- Technology: can the prototype or intervention be built and tested?
- Time: are recruitment, development, testing, analysis, and revision schedulable?
- Resources: are devices, connectivity, licenses, budget, and facilities available?
- Scope: can the work be completed without reducing the research to a feature list?

A high-value study that is infeasible should be narrowed or redesigned, not described with optimistic assumptions.

## 5. Master roadmap design

Use a dependency-aware work breakdown structure. Each task must have:

- Unique ID and phase.
- Action verb and specific output.
- Prerequisite task IDs.
- Responsible person or role, if known.
- Effort estimate and confidence.
- Start/due milestone or relative sequence.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Evidence of completion.
- Risks and approval dependencies.

Represent dates as provisional until confirmed. Schedule backward from a known submission/defense deadline and include review/rework buffers. Do not allocate all available time to initial drafting.

### Suggested default phases

| Phase | Main outputs | Exit condition |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Reconnaissance | Study brief, artifact inventory | Existing facts and gaps are documented |
| 1. Scoping | Feasibility, scope, study architecture | Scope and study type are defensible |
| 2. Evidence | Search protocol, source log, evidence matrix, synthesis | Claims and gap are supported and traceable |
| 3. Research design | Objectives, questions, framework, methods | Alignment audit passes |
| 4. Approval | Ethics, permissions, instruments, protocol | Required approvals are obtained before fieldwork |
| 5. Development/collection | System or data collection records | Protocol execution is documented |
| 6. Analysis | Clean dataset, coding, analysis output | Results answer the questions |
| 7. Writing | Chapters, references, tables/figures | Draft passes internal review |
| 8. Validation | Technical, methodological, and citation audits | Critical defects resolved |
| 9. Defense | Presentation, Q&A bank, final files | Claims can be defended with evidence |

Adjust this sequence to the actual study. Some activities iterate, but approvals must precede restricted data collection.

## 6. Governance and change control

Maintain:

- **Decision log:** decisions, options, rationale, approval, and downstream effects.
- **Risk register:** likelihood, impact, trigger, owner, mitigation, contingency.
- **Change log:** scope, question, method, instrument, timeline, or system changes.
- **Progress log:** completed outputs, evidence, blockers, and next actions.

When a question or objective changes, inspect every dependent component. A seemingly small wording change can affect the framework, sampling, instrument, analysis, ethics submission, and chapter drafts.

## 7. Milestone acceptance

A milestone is complete only when its output satisfies explicit criteria. “Literature review researched” is not an acceptance criterion. Better criteria include: all included studies have verified metadata; the search log records databases and dates; the evidence matrix is populated; themes are synthesized; and the research gap is supported by the sources included in scope.

## 8. Avoid common planning failures

- Choosing a technology stack before validating user needs and constraints.
- Writing the problem statement around the features the team wants to build.
- Claiming novelty based only on the absence of an identical local application.
- Selecting a sample size because it is convenient without a rationale.
- Writing an instrument before defining constructs and variables.
- Treating a supervisor's informal suggestion as institutional approval.
- Leaving analysis decisions until after data collection.
- Scheduling no time for failed tests, recruitment delays, revisions, or defense preparation.
