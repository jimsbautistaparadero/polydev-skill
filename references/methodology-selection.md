# Methodology, Sampling, and Instrument Design

## 1. Start with the question

Choose a design because it can answer the research question, not because it is familiar or easy to write about. Separate the broad research approach from the specific design and from the data-collection technique.

| Question type | Candidate design | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| What is happening / how common? | Descriptive quantitative, survey, records analysis | Structured observations, survey, administrative records |
| How do people experience or interpret it? | Qualitative descriptive, phenomenology, case study, ethnography (when justified) | Interviews, observations, documents |
| Why/how does a process work? | Case study, process evaluation, qualitative or mixed methods | Multiple sources, process records, interviews |
| Does an intervention change an outcome? | Experimental or quasi-experimental, with suitable comparator and timing | Baseline/follow-up or comparison data |
| How are variables associated? | Correlational or observational analytic design | Defined variables and appropriate sampling/analysis |
| What should be designed and how should it be evaluated? | Design science, user-centered design, design-and-evaluation study | Requirements, design artifacts, demonstrations, evaluation evidence |
| What does the existing evidence show? | Systematic, scoping, integrative, or narrative review | Searchable literature corpus and explicit synthesis method |

A method name does not guarantee rigor. Explain the procedure and the conditions under which it is credible.

## 2. Quantitative design

Specify:

- Population and operational population definition.
- Sampling frame and access to it.
- Sampling method and actual selection procedure.
- Sample-size rationale based on objective, design, expected effect/precision, population, feasibility, and available guidance.
- Variables, measurement scale, source, coding, and timing.
- Instrument source, adaptation, translation, and pilot process.
- Analysis plan and assumptions.

Do not use a formula mechanically without stating its assumptions. A sample-size calculation depends on the target estimand, design, expected prevalence/effect, precision/power, confidence level, design effect, and nonresponse assumptions. If key inputs are unknown, present a range or scenario analysis and mark it provisional.

## 3. Qualitative design

Specify:

- Research tradition/design and why it fits.
- Participant selection logic and information-rich criteria.
- Recruitment and consent process.
- Interview/observation guide and piloting.
- Recording, transcription, translation, and secure storage.
- Coding and analytic approach.
- Reflexivity and positionality where relevant.
- Credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability strategies as appropriate.
- How contradictory cases and saturation/information power are handled.

Do not claim a universal sample size for qualitative research. Justify adequacy through design, specificity, depth, analytic purpose, and available time.

## 4. Mixed methods

State why one method alone is insufficient. Specify:

- Which strand is first and why.
- Whether strands run sequentially or concurrently.
- Priority of each strand.
- How the sample/data sets relate.
- At which point integration occurs.
- How conflicting findings will be handled.
- What integrated inference the study expects to produce.

“Using surveys and interviews” is not a sufficient mixed-methods design unless the strands are intentionally integrated.

## 5. Sampling integrity

Distinguish probability sampling (e.g., simple random, stratified, cluster) from non-probability sampling (e.g., purposive, convenience, quota, snowball). Define the sampling frame. State who is excluded and why. Explain the implications for bias and generalizability/transferability.

For a census or complete enumeration, define the eligible population and explain how all eligible units will be approached and nonresponse recorded.

## 6. Instrument development

For every instrument:

1. Define the construct, variable, or process being measured.
2. Identify dimensions and operational definitions.
3. Map every item/question to a dimension and research question/objective.
4. Identify source items or rationale for new items.
5. Specify response options, scoring, skip logic, and missing-response treatment.
6. Review language, reading level, cultural context, accessibility, and translation.
7. Obtain expert review or appropriate content validation.
8. Pilot with a suitable group, avoiding contamination of the main sample when necessary.
9. Revise and version the instrument.
10. Record reliability/validity evidence appropriate to the instrument and design.

Expert review alone does not establish construct validity or reliability. A pilot tests feasibility and clarity; it does not automatically validate the instrument.

## 7. Research ethics and permissions

Before collecting data, determine whether formal ethics review, organizational permission, data-owner authorization, parental/guardian consent, or other approval is required. The institution decides its process. Do not assume an academic capstone is exempt.

Plan informed consent, voluntary participation, withdrawal, risk minimization, privacy, confidentiality, data minimization, secure storage, retention/deletion, access rights, and reporting safeguards. Avoid collecting sensitive personal information unless necessary and approved.

## 8. Review-study designs

- **Narrative review:** critical synthesis with flexible search; disclose scope and selection approach.
- **Scoping review:** maps concepts, evidence types, and gaps in a broad field; use a defined protocol and suitable reporting guidance.
- **Systematic review:** answers a focused question with explicit, reproducible search, selection, appraisal, and synthesis methods.
- **Rapid review:** streamlines some steps due to time limits; report the shortcuts and their implications.
- **Integrative review:** combines varied evidence types using a clear synthesis logic.

Do not call a narrative literature chapter a systematic review unless it meets the methodological requirements.

## 9. Method selection record

For each serious candidate design, record fit to research questions, evidence availability, feasibility, threats to validity, ethics, analytical burden, and what claims it can support. Recommend the strongest feasible design and state what it cannot establish.
