# Evidence Quality, Source Verification, and Citation Integrity

## 1. Match the source to the claim

A source is useful only if it supports the specific claim being made. A peer-reviewed article about a different population may be strong evidence for a general mechanism but weak evidence for a local prevalence estimate. A vendor page may accurately document a product feature but is not independent evidence that the feature improves outcomes.

## 2. Practical source hierarchy

Use the following as a starting point; context and claim type can change the ordering.

1. Primary empirical studies, original datasets, official statistics, official standards, legislation, and original technical specifications.
2. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, high-quality scoping reviews, and authoritative evidence syntheses.
3. Peer-reviewed theoretical, methodological, and discipline-specific articles.
4. University theses and institutional repositories, assessed for methods and review quality.
5. Government, intergovernmental, professional association, and established research-institute reports.
6. Reputable technical documentation and vendor documentation for claims about the vendor's own system.
7. Credible professional or news reporting for context, discovery, and time-sensitive developments.
8. Blogs, forums, social posts, and promotional pages only as leads or as evidence of public discourse—not as a substitute for scholarly or official evidence.

This is not a universal ranking. An official standard is authoritative for its own requirements; a primary study is needed for an empirical outcome; documentation is often best for a software API's current behavior.

## 3. Verify bibliographic identity

For every cited source, verify as many fields as are applicable:

- Author names and order.
- Publication year and online-first versus issue year.
- Exact title and subtitle.
- Journal/conference/book/report name.
- Volume, issue, page range, article number, and publisher.
- DOI or stable URL.
- Publication type and status (published, accepted, preprint, thesis, report).

DOI checks should resolve to the same work. Crossref or another registry can corroborate metadata but may itself contain errors. The publisher's version of record is usually the best final identity check. Record metadata conflicts rather than silently choosing one.

## 4. Evidence access status

Label each source:

- `full-text reviewed`
- `abstract only`
- `metadata only`
- `secondary citation only`

Do not present a detailed account of the methods or findings of an abstract-only paper as though the full text was examined. Do not cite a source that you have not identified well enough to verify it exists.

## 5. Critical appraisal prompts

Assess quality relative to the question:

- Is the research question clear?
- Is the design suitable?
- Is the sample/data source appropriate and described?
- Are inclusion criteria and recruitment transparent?
- Are measures valid and reliable for this population and context?
- Are confounders, biases, and missing data addressed?
- Is analysis appropriate and transparent?
- Do conclusions match the results?
- Are limitations disclosed?
- Are conflicts of interest or funding relevant?
- Is the context transferable to the current study?

Do not reduce appraisal to a single numeric score unless a recognized tool is appropriate and applied correctly.

## 6. Citation behavior

- Cite the original source for an important claim whenever accessible.
- Cite secondary sources as secondary sources; do not imply the original paper was read.
- Put citations close to the claims they support.
- Use the institution's required citation style. If no style is specified, ask or state a provisional default before formatting a whole bibliography.
- Never manufacture page numbers, issue numbers, DOI strings, quotations, or author lists.
- Avoid citation padding: a long cluster of citations is not a substitute for explaining how the evidence supports the point.
- Ensure all in-text citations and reference entries correspond.
- Preserve exact titles and proper names; do not translate article titles unless the style requires a bracketed translation.
- For web sources, capture the organization/author, date, page title, URL, and access date where the selected style requires it.

## 7. Reference formatting audit

For APA 7, check author order, year, sentence-case article titles, italicization, journal/volume formatting, issue formatting, page range/article number, DOI in URL form, and reference-list alphabetization. For conference proceedings, theses, government reports, and web pages, use the corresponding APA source-type rules. Do not force every source into a journal-article pattern.

## 8. Avoid misleading literature statements

Replace broad unsupported phrases:

- “There are no existing studies” → specify the databases, search dates, terms, and scope, then state that the search identified limited evidence within those boundaries.
- “The system is unique” → compare relevant systems on defined features, context, and evaluation evidence; explain the specific distinction.
- “Most studies prove” → report the number and nature of studies and describe consistency, quality, and limitations.
- “The method is accurate” → state the test, metric, sample, and observed result that support the claim.
- “The instrument is validated” → identify the validation evidence, population, construct, and whether adaptation requires revalidation.

## 9. Evidence traceability

Every major claim in the problem statement, literature synthesis, framework, and discussion should be traceable to:

- A source ID in the evidence matrix.
- The exact supporting page/section/table/figure if available.
- A note explaining which part of the claim is supported.

For claims based on multiple sources, record the joint basis and note any disagreement.

## 10. Red flags requiring review

- DOI resolves to a different title.
- Journal title, year, or author list differs across records.
- Citation cannot be found outside AI-generated text.
- A paper is cited for a claim it did not investigate.
- Search results were read as if they were full texts.
- A publisher or repository page has retracted/corrected the work.
- A preprint is presented as peer-reviewed.
- The reference list contains fabricated pagination or invented article numbers.
